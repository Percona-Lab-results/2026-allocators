Data source: run_allocator_perf_suite.sh results in suite-rockperf2. Phases per run: steady load → idle → regrow. Time-series lines are the mean across repetitions (solid = THP enabled, dashed = THP disabled; color = allocator). Bars are the median across repetitions.

Verdict

Setup: MyRocks (RocksDB block cache 48 GB), 400 warehouses, HammerDB TPC-C (80 VUs) plus bash malloc-stress clients; steady-only runs (10 m rampup + 30 m measured); 6 run instances per configuration (3 from the initial invocation, 3+ from reruns with the fixed TPS-counter collector, so this report includes TPS-over-time). Notes: performance_schema does not instrument RocksDB’s internals, so compare RSS directly (the “overhead” metric is not meaningful here); thp and nothp halves ran concurrently on separate hosts, so THP percentages carry a host variable.

  • Confirms the MyRocks ranking at double the sample count: jemalloc 3.6 + THP wins by a wide margin — 8,643 TPS, +25 % over the slowest configuration and +13 % over the next-best THP one. It remains the only allocator whose memory gets nearly fully huge-page-backed (53.5 GB AnonHugePages vs 6.6–17 GB; its 2–4 MB-aligned chunks are khugepaged-friendly).
  • Without THP: jemalloc 3.6 (7,715) > glibc (7,558) > jemalloc 5.3 (7,077) > tcmalloc (6,896) — the InnoDB winner is again the MyRocks loser on throughput.
  • RSS spreads by 23 %: tcmalloc 49.0 GB (leanest), jemalloc 3.6 50.8–52.3 GB, glibc 56.6 GB, jemalloc 5.3 59.6–60.5 GB (the largest, while also slow).
  • The TPS timeline (new in this dataset) shows the gap is still widening at the 40-minute mark: jemalloc 5.3 degrades through the run (nothp: 7,677 TPS at 15 min → 7,050 at 39 min, −8 % — consistent with progressive fragmentation of RocksDB’s allocation pattern), while tcmalloc is still improving at the end (6,945 → 7,269, +5 %), and jemalloc 3.6 and glibc are roughly flat. The 30-minute window therefore flatters jemalloc 5.3 and slightly undersells tcmalloc — longer runs would likely widen 5.3’s deficit and narrow tcmalloc’s.

Bottom line: for MyRocks run jemalloc 3.6 with THP always — fastest by a wide margin, mid-pack RSS, flat over time. jemalloc 5.3 is the worst choice here (slow, 23 % more RSS than tcmalloc, and still degrading when the run ends); tcmalloc trades throughput for the smallest footprint; glibc is mediocre and barely benefits from THP. Recommended follow-ups: 60–90 minute steady runs to capture the post-30-minute trends, and jemalloc 5.3 with opt.thp/2 MB extent alignment to test whether it can recover the 3.6 behavior.

MySQL TPM over time (mean across reps)

Com_commit + Com_rollback per minute (the counters behind HammerDB's "MySQL TPM"), sampled every 30 s. Shows rampup, steady plateau, the idle gap and the regrow plateau; a lower second plateau indicates work the idle phase left behind (data growth, purge backlog). Requires the txn-counter log; runs from older suite versions are not shown here.

RSS over time (mean across reps)

mysqld resident memory through the steady → idle → regrow phases. Idle phase is shaded; a drop there is memory the allocator returned to the OS, and a higher regrow plateau than steady is the fragmentation ratchet.

Allocator overhead over time (mean across reps)

RSS minus performance_schema tracked bytes: memory the process holds beyond what the server accounts for — allocator caches, fragmentation and untracked allocations. Lower and flatter is better.

Mapping count over time (mean across reps)

Number of /proc/pid/maps entries — address-space fragmentation and kernel VMA bookkeeping cost.

THP-backed memory over time (mean across reps)

AnonHugePages from smaps_rollup — how much anonymous memory each allocator keeps on transparent huge pages (thp runs only; nothp runs sit at zero by design).

RSS released during idle (median of reps)

RSS at end of steady minus end of idle: how much memory the allocator gave back to the OS when load stopped. Higher is better.

Regrow ratchet (median of reps)

RSS at end of regrow minus end of steady: growth of the plateau after an idle/regrow cycle — a fragmentation indicator. Lower is better.
