Data source: run_allocator_perf_suite.sh results in suite-allocperf3-800-warehouses. Phases per run: steady load → idle → regrow. Time-series lines are the mean across repetitions (solid = THP enabled, dashed = THP disabled; color = allocator). Bars are the median across repetitions.

Verdict

Setup: 800 warehouses (~80 GB) against a 48 GB buffer pool — the dataset exceeds the pool by ~1.7×, so the workload is partially I/O-bound (~9.7k TPS vs ~11.1k for the same configurations fully cached). HammerDB TPC-C plus malloc-stress clients; phases steady → idle → regrow; 3 repetitions. THP engaged partially (28–32 GB AnonHugePages in thp runs).

  • Throughput is a statistical tie: the full spread from slowest to fastest configuration is 0.78 %, with rep noise of 0.2–0.6 %. Under I/O wait the allocator’s CPU cost compresses to near-invisibility. The THP gain also collapses to +0.2–0.7 % (vs ~+2 % when cached).
  • Memory still differentiates clearly: glibc again shows ~2.5 GB untracked overhead (vs 1.7–1.9 GB for the rest) and a ~600–700 MB/cycle plateau ratchet — an order of magnitude above jemalloc 3.6 (59 MB). jemalloc 5.3 with THP shows its known idle-phase growth (~510 MB). tcmalloc has the steadiest profile.
  • Methodological lesson: letting the dataset exceed the buffer pool dilutes the throughput signal this suite exists to measure — page I/O adds noise and no malloc traffic. For allocator comparisons keep the dataset cached; test I/O pressure as a separate question.

Bottom line: in I/O-mixed conditions allocator choice does not measurably move throughput; the decision rests on memory behavior, where tcmalloc (most stable) and jemalloc 3.6 (leanest) win and glibc’s compounding growth again makes it the one to avoid. (Caveat: thp and nothp halves ran concurrently on separate hosts.)
